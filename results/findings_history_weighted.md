# FINDINGS history_weighted (history-weighted per-sender metering) - READING RULES FIXED BEFORE THE RESULTS (written 2026-11-20 08:20; results are appended after report_history_weighted.md, verify_history_weighted.out and a blind review)
## What this experiment is (framing, from the blind design reviews)
It moves from checking the existing RAAC admission design to a new research direction: admission that differentiates service by account history. It is NOT an attack detector. The question is whether account history is useful differentiating information at all, which service it gives to whom, and what an attacker with prior traffic gets.
## Reading rules
1. History effect versus budget effect: HistBucket is read against SenderLow10 (flat 10 KB/s for all accounts) and SenderHigh400 (flat 400 KB/s for all) and SenderBucket (flat 100 KB/s), not only against none; the aggregate allowed heavy rate per block (table in the report) is shown with the results because the total budget grows with the number of accounts (Y2: 12 attack accounts).
2. History = ordinary requests SEEN by the gate (submissions), not on-chain inclusions; rejected heavy requests do not count; no decay. The bypass cost is stated as the prior traffic used (Y3: 10 s / 33 requests; Y5: 60 s / 200 requests), not as an economic cost. Sentence to use: "accounts with the stated prior traffic bypassed it".
3. Y6 (fresh legitimate heavy senders) is a main result: if it throttles fresh legitimate heavy senders and passes pre-aged attackers (Y5), the policy is described as differential service by account history, not as attack detection.
4. Agreement of Y1/Y3/Y5 with the expectations written beforehand is not presented as an independent prediction (the design encodes the mechanism); Y2 is compared with Y1 only qualitatively (benign background load differs by block because silent attack accounts send no ordinary traffic).
5. Not allowed: distinguishes spam from legitimate traffic; Sybil-resistant; attackers must pay a meaningful cost; protects against adaptive attackers; real recovered-address behaviour; production use. Not tested: stake/deposit/authenticated capabilities (the reviewer's alternative), history decay, compromised or aged-for-days accounts.
## H1B reading rule (Nethermind 1,024 MiB, benign only)
Death without attack = "this heap is insufficient for this benign workload and configuration" (observed: 4/4 died at 108-130 s); death only under attack would not identify whether the character of the attack or the added volume matters. Failures during the load and exceptions/failures after it are reported separately (here all deaths are inside the 240 s load).

## Reading (what the numbers show)
- Budget assignment by account: HistBucket behaves like the 10 KB/s flat bucket for accounts without history and like the 400 KB/s flat bucket for accounts with history (history and budget are not independent effects: the history rule works by assigning different budgets). Y1/Y4: attack blocked 0.965 (as flat 10) while established look-alikes are not rejected (as flat 400) - flat 10 rejects 318 / 386 look-alikes and flat 400 blocks nothing. Y2: HistBucket blocks 0.791 of a fragmented fresh attack that the flat 100 bucket does not block at all (0.000) although its aggregate allowed rate is higher (2,520 vs 1,800): the outcome differs with how the budget is assigned per account, not with the total.
- Accounts with prior ordinary requests bypass it: Y3 (33 requests per account) and Y5 (200 requests per account): attack block 0.000. In Y5 the flat 100 bucket still blocks 0.693, so HistBucket is worse than the flat bucket against pre-aged accounts. No decay: prior activity is a permanent privilege in this design.
- Y6 (main result): look-alike accounts without history are throttled exactly like the attack (318 of 402 rejected, attack block 0.965, identical to flat 10): the policy gives service by account history, not by transaction legitimacy.
## What the benign-receipt column can and cannot say
In Y2, Y3 and Y6 the benign receipts are identical in every seed and arm group (15,120; 15,516; 18,882): with 12 silent attack accounts or silent fresh look-alikes the background load is light, every benign request is receipted, the metric is at its ceiling and says nothing about the policies there; in those blocks only attack block and look-alike rejection are informative. In Y1/Y4/Y5 receipts rise with the heavy volume withheld (none 16,090 / 16,227 / 14,704; HistBucket 21,979 / 22,103 / 14,615): this reflects admitted heavy volume, not a quality-of-service guarantee, and not user fairness. Four seeds give identical counters in many cells (deterministic setup), so intervals are not informative about variability outside this generator.
## Allowed wording
"In this deterministic benchmark, history-weighted metering blocked most heavy requests from accounts without prior ordinary-request history while admitting established accounts, including a legitimate heavy sender at 327 KB/s, and fragmented fresh accounts were still throttled (0.79) where a flat per-sender bucket was bypassed. Accounts with 33 or more prior ordinary requests bypassed it, a flat bucket was more effective than the history rule against pre-aged accounts, and legitimate heavy senders without history were throttled like the attack (318 of 402)."
## Not allowed
Attack or intent detection; Sybil resistance or an attacker cost (only the prior traffic used is reported); protection of new legitimate users; production security; fairness; any generalization beyond this generator and its worker-account mapping; reading benign receipts as protection (ceiling in Y2/Y3/Y6, admitted-volume effect elsewhere).
## One-line conclusion
Account history is useful differentiating information against fresh accounts, but it separates accounts, not attacks: it fails against accounts that have bought a small amount of history, penalizes new legitimate senders, and in one case is worse than a flat budget.

## Wording corrections after a second blind review (2026-11-20)
- Contribution sentence to use: "Differential per-account budgets by account history were advantageous when fresh attack accounts and established legitimate accounts were separated, but were vulnerable to prior history accumulation and to new legitimate users."
- Not established by this experiment: that a scarce signal (stake, authentication) is a necessary condition; other signals (behaviour, cost observations) are possible. What can be said: the tested features and account history alone did not separate adaptive attackers reliably.
- The positive result (Y1, Y2, Y4: attack blocking together with legitimate-heavy protection, which none of the flat buckets achieved) is real but conditional on legitimate accounts having history and attack accounts being fresh. In the paper this experiment is a follow-up exploration of selectivity and its limits, not a solution, and it is not claimed to resolve control stability or fair-baseline comparison.
